# Concept engines

A concept engine is a deliberate move that turns a subject into an idea. Take the subject, run it quickly through three or four of these, and keep the result you can already see as a picture. Drop the clever ones that need explaining.

## The engines

### Metaphor

Show the feeling or meaning through something else that shares its shape.

- Burnout: a matchbook with a single match left
- Grief: a house where one room has its furniture under dust sheets
- Starting over: a pair of shoes too clean for the muddy path in front of them

Best for abstract requests: a feeling, a theme, an article illustration. Stay away from the stock set (lightbulbs for ideas, chess for strategy, handshakes, mazes, puzzle pieces, icebergs). They have been used so often that nobody sees them any more.

### Collision

Two things that don't share a world, in one frame, treated as completely normal.

- A koi pond filling a parking space, the painted lines still visible under the water
- A grand piano in a wheat field after rain
- A laundromat on the seabed, the machines still turning

Render it deadpan: realistic light, no emphasis, no glow. The matter-of-fact treatment is what makes it strange.

### Scale

Make the small monumental or the vast tiny.

- A snail shell as a cathedral, with visitors on the steps
- A mountain range inside a soup bowl
- A commuter crossing a keyboard the size of a plaza

Give the eye a ruler: a person, a chair, a coin. Without a familiar object, scale doesn't register.

### Material swap

Build the subject out of the wrong stuff.

- A thunderstorm in embroidery thread on linen, the lightning in metallic floss
- A sports car carved from a block of butter
- A portrait assembled from pressed flowers

The material brings its own texture, physics and associations. Describe how it behaves: thread puckers the cloth, butter softens at the edges, petals go translucent where they overlap.

### Viewpoint

Look from where nobody stands.

- From inside: the fridge, the letterbox, the piano
- From directly above: the everyday becomes pattern
- From the ground, at the height of a child or an animal
- From very far away, so the subject is a speck in its setting
- From the object's side: what the umbrella sees

### Time

Choose a moment other than the obvious one, or show more than one.

- The moment before: the diver still on the board, toes over the edge
- The moment after: confetti on an empty floor, chairs pushed back
- Long exposure: motion as streaks, a crowd as ghosts, the sea as mist
- Many moments at once: the sun at each hour, strung across one sky
- Age: the same thing in a hundred years, overgrown or worn smooth

### Absence

Show the subject by what it leaves behind: shadows, reflections, footprints, imprints, outlines in dust.

- A dog: the worn patch on the sofa and a lead on its hook
- A cellist: a chair, a music stand, rosin dust on the floorboards
- A crowd: flattened grass and one lost glove

Strong for memory, loss, affection and quiet. Often more moving than showing the thing itself.

### Genre transplant

Render a modern or mundane subject in the conventions of an unrelated tradition.

- Rush hour as an illuminated manuscript, gold leaf on the brake lights
- A server room as a Dutch Golden Age still life
- A supermarket aisle as a ukiyo-e woodblock
- A football match on a Greek black-figure vase

Commit fully to the tradition's materials and rules: the flattened perspective, the border ornament, the limited pigments. Half-commitment looks like a filter.

### Constraint

Remove options until a style appears.

- Two inks only
- Nothing but circles
- One continuous line
- Only the shadow, never the object
- Only lettering

Especially good for posters, marks, patterns and anything graphic.

### Inversion

Flip the one thing everyone expects.

- A lighthouse that casts a beam of darkness across a bright sea
- A beach in deep winter, deckchairs under snow
- A desert cactus wrapped in a knitted jumper

Flip one thing and keep everything else ordinary, so the flip is what the viewer notices.

### Tone against subject

Treat the subject with the opposite of its usual mood.

- A monster painted tenderly, in the manner of a mother-and-child portrait
- A birthday party under flat grey office light
- A demolition site in sugared-almond pastels

### Specificity

Less a source of surprise than a cure for blandness. Replace the category with the particular.

- "An old man" becomes a retired ferry captain still wearing the company jumper, one elbow darned
- "A kitchen" becomes a 1970s rental kitchen with avocado tiles and one cupboard door hung slightly crooked
- "A cat" becomes a one-eared ginger tom asleep in a box of tangerines at a market stall

Specific details imply a life outside the frame. Use this on top of any other engine.

## Choosing between ideas

- **Can you already see it?** If it takes a paragraph to explain, it will take more than one image to show.
- **Does it suit the purpose?** A nursery print and an album cover want different degrees of strangeness.
- **Is it one idea?** If it has an "and", cut it in half.
- **Is it your second or third idea?** First ideas are everybody's first ideas.

## Offering three directions

Make the three differ in engine, medium and mood all at once. Three linocuts with three compositions are one direction. A spread that usually works: one quiet, one graphic, one strange.

"An image about coffee for my café wall":

1. **Quiet (absence).** The ring a cup leaves behind, enlarged until it fills the frame like a planet seen from orbit. Sepia ink wash on cotton paper.
2. **Graphic (constraint).** Two-colour screenprint in cream and espresso brown. Steam rises from a cup as one continuous line that becomes the skyline of the neighbourhood.
3. **Strange (scale).** A tiny rowing boat crossing a flat white, the latte art as its wake. Macro photograph, shallow focus, morning window light.

"Something for my daughter's room, she loves whales":

1. **Quiet (viewpoint).** From far above, a whale's shadow passing under a very small red boat. Gouache, three blues and one red.
2. **Graphic (genre transplant).** A whale in the manner of a vintage botanical chart, labelled parts and all, with barnacles drawn like flowers. Hand-tinted engraving on cream paper.
3. **Strange (collision).** A whale drifting between rooftops at dusk like a slow airship, lit windows below. Cut-paper collage in dusky violet and warm yellow.

Present directions in a line or two each. The point is to let the user choose quickly, not to read a brief.
